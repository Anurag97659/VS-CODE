The Numbers Game

Two players, a blackboard, and one repeated subtraction. Why the entire outcome hinges on whether one number is at least φ ≈ 1.618 times the other — proved from scratch, made exact in integers, and turned into an O(range) counting algorithm.

01

The rules, precisely

Arya and Bran are given two positive integers A and B written on a blackboard. Arya moves first, then they alternate. On a turn, a player must do exactly one of:

  • replace A with A − k·B for some positive integer k, or
  • replace B with B − k·A for some positive integer k.

There is no "pass" — a move is compulsory, and the numbers must strictly change. The first player who makes either number drop to zero or below, loses.

A pair (A, B) is called a winning position if the player about to move (in the actual contest, that's whoever's turn it is when the pair is written down) can force a win no matter how the opponent replies. The contest's real question, asked later, is a counting one:

The exact task
Given A1 ≤ A ≤ A2 and B1 ≤ B ≤ B2, count how many pairs (A, B) in that rectangle are winning positions for whoever moves first.
02

Positions: winning vs. losing

This is standard combinatorial game theory vocabulary, worth pinning down before any golden ratio shows up:

  • N-position ("Next player wins"): the player about to move has at least one move leading to a P-position for the opponent.
  • P-position ("Previous player wins", i.e. the mover loses): every legal move leads to an N-position for the opponent — or there is no legal move that avoids losing immediately.

Since a player only ever subtracts a multiple of the smaller-ish number from the other, and is only ever forced to create a non-positive number when they have literally no other choice, the base case is clean:

Base case
If A = B, every legal move sets one of them to A − k·A ≤ 0. There is no escape. (A, A) is always a P-position — the player to move loses.

Everything else is built from this base case by recursion: a pair is a P-position exactly when every reachable pair is an N-position, and an N-position exactly when some reachable pair is a P-position.

03

Small cases, worked by hand

Take (2, 3). The only sensible move is on the larger number (subtracting a multiple of 3 from 2 is instantly fatal), and the only multiple of 2 you can subtract from 3 without going non-positive is k = 1: 3 − 1·2 = 1, giving (2, 1), i.e. (1, 2).

Now, is (1, 2) winning for whoever faces it? From (1, 2) a player can play 2 − 1·1 = 1, reaching (1, 1) — the base case, a P-position. So (1, 2) is an N-position (winning for its mover).

Back to (2, 3): its only move leads to (1, 2), which is winning for the opponent. Since that's the only option, (2, 3) has no move to a P-position — it is itself a P-position (losing for its mover).

Repeating this by hand for every pair from 1 to 10 gives the following map (each cell is scored from the point of view of whoever is about to move on that pair):

Two things jump out immediately:

  • The dark (losing) cells form diagonal bands hugging the line b = a.
  • Each band gets proportionally wider as a grows, not wider by a fixed amount — a straight-line boundary, not a curve.

That second observation is the whole ballgame: it says the losing region is bounded by rays through the origin, i.e. by a fixed ratio b/a. The question becomes: which ratio?

04

Why the golden ratio appears

Suppose a < b and think about what happens for large numbers, ignoring integer rounding for a moment — treat a and b as real numbers and ask: past what ratio r = b/a can the mover always force a win?

From (a, b), the mover's only useful moves are on b: subtract some multiple of a. Two extreme strategies matter:

  1. If b ≥ 2a, the mover can play b − a and land exactly on (a, a) — the P-position base case. Win, immediately. So every ratio r ≥ 2 is winning.
  2. If a < b < 2a, the only reachable pair (other than an instant loss) is (b − a, a), i.e. the roles swap and the new ratio becomes a / (b − a). The mover is stuck with whatever that forces — there's no choice at all.

So for ratios between 1 and 2, winning or losing is decided entirely by the new ratio a / (b − a). Write r = b/a. Then the follow-up ratio is:

r' = a / (b − a) = 1 / (r − 1)

Now here's the key idea: the threshold ratio φ that separates winning from losing must be a fixed point of this transformation — because just below the threshold you're forced into the *complement* of the threshold region, and consistency requires the boundary to map to itself under r ↦ 1/(r−1). Setting r = 1/(r − 1):

r(r − 1) = 1  ⟺  r² − r − 1 = 0

The positive root of this quadratic is exactly the golden ratio:

The threshold

φ = (1 + √5) / 2 ≈ 1.6180339887…

The full (rigorous) argument runs by strong induction on a + b: claim — for a ≤ b, (a, b) is an N-position (mover wins) if and only if b > φ·a.

  • Base case a = b: ratio is 1 < φ, and indeed it's a P-position. ✓
  • If b > φ·a: one can always choose k = ⌊b/a⌋ (or one less, to dodge landing on a multiple exactly) so that the resulting b' = b − k·a satisfies b' ≤ φ·a, i.e. lands the opponent in a P-position by the induction hypothesis. A short case check (whether b mod a already works, or you must back off by one multiple of a) confirms such a k always exists — this is exactly the "floor and one-off" bookkeeping the algorithm needs.
  • If b ≤ φ·a: every reachable b' = b − k·a for k ≥ 1 lands with a > b', and one can show the resulting ratio a / b' always exceeds φ, handing the opponent a winning position, by the induction hypothesis. So the mover has no escape — it's a P-position.

This is the same φ that governs Fibonacci numbers, continued fractions, and the "worst case" of the Euclidean algorithm — unsurprising, since this game *is* essentially the subtractive Euclidean algorithm, just scored by parity of steps and with a strict "don't touch zero" rule bolted on.

06

An exact integer test (no floating point)

φ is irrational, so multiplying by 1.6180339887… in floating point risks rounding error exactly where it matters most — near the boundary, for numbers up to 109. The lecture's emphasis on floor and max(0, …) bookkeeping is really about avoiding this trap. There are two clean, exact ways to do it:

6.1 — A single yes/no check, using only integers

φ solves x² = x + 1. So "M/m > φ" (for M = max(a,b), m = min(a,b)) can be tested by squaring both sides of M > φ·m, using φ·m satisfying the same quadratic relationship — which collapses to a single polynomial inequality with no square roots and no φ at all:

# (a, b) is a WINNING position for whoever moves first, iff:
M*M > M*m + m*m   # where m = min(a,b), M = max(a,b)

This never has a tie for positive integers (equality would require M/m = φ, impossible since φ is irrational) — so this single line is a complete, exact classifier. This is what generated every colored cell in the diagrams above.

6.2 — A threshold formula, for counting many pairs at once

To count pairs efficiently (Section 8) we need, for a fixed a, the smallest b > a that wins. That means finding the smallest integer b with b > a·φ. Using φ = (1+√5)/2:

2b > a + a√5  ⟺  2b − a > a√5

Let s = ⌊a·√5⌋, computed exactly via integer square root (isqrt(5·a²) — again, no floating point). Since a√5 is irrational, it sits strictly between s and s+1, so the smallest integer exceeding it is always s+1:

Exact threshold

b_min(a) = ⌈(a + s + 1) / 2⌉ = ⌊(a + s + 2) / 2⌋,   where s = isqrt(5·a²)

Every b ≥ b_min(a) is winning (as the larger number); every b with a < b < b_min(a) is losing. This formula was checked by brute force for a = 1..12:

07

Fibonacci pairs oscillate around φ

A pleasant, non-obvious subtlety worth knowing for an exam: it is not true that all consecutive Fibonacci pairs are losing positions. Since F(n+1)/F(n) converges to φ by alternating above and below it, consecutive Fibonacci pairs alternate between winning and losing:

The alternation is a direct consequence of the identity Fₙ₊₁Fₙ₋₁ − Fₙ² = (−1)ⁿ — the sign flips with parity, and that sign is exactly what tips the ratio to one side of φ or the other. This is a good talking point if an examiner asks "are Fibonacci pairs always losing positions?" — the honest, precise answer is no, they alternate.

08

Counting winning pairs in a range

The actual contest question: count winning (A, B) with A ∈ [A1, A2], B ∈ [B1, B2]. Split into the two disjoint cases (a pair can't satisfy both, and A = B never wins, so there's no double-counting):

  • Case 1 — B is the larger number and wins: B ≥ b_min(A).
  • Case 2 — A is the larger number and wins: A ≥ b_min(B) (same formula, roles swapped).

For each case, sweep the fixed variable across its range and count how many values of the other variable clear the threshold, clamped into the given interval with max(0, …):

for a in range(A1, A2+1):
    lo = max(B1, b_min(a))
    if lo <= B2:
        total += B2 - lo + 1       # count of valid b in this row

and symmetrically sweeping b across [B1, B2] for Case 2. Each sweep is O(range size), and each step is O(1) (an isqrt call) — comfortably fast for ranges up to a million or more, exactly the efficiency the lecture claims. This was checked against a full brute-force double loop across 200 random small rectangles — every single one matched exactly, and the 5×5 grid gives 14 winning pairs by both methods.

Pitfall to avoid
Don't forget that A = B pairs are always losing and must never be counted in either case — the threshold formulas above automatically exclude them since b_min(a) > a always, but it's worth stating explicitly if you're deriving this on an exam.
09

The official example, re-derived

The problem statement's own worked example starts at (12, 51) and ends with Bran losing. Let's confirm that with the exact integer test rather than trusting the narrative:

M = 51, m = 12  ⟹  M² = 2601,   M·m + m² = 612 + 144 = 756

2601 > 756  ⟹  WINNING position — matches Arya winning. ✓

Notice the game trace in the problem statement doesn't even need to be the optimal line of play for this conclusion to hold — the classification of (12, 51) as winning is a property of the starting pair itself, independent of which particular (valid) sequence of moves gets played out.

10

Reference implementation

A complete, verified Python solution — single-position check, threshold formula, and the range-counting routine used above:

import math

def is_winning(a, b):
    """True iff (a, b) is a winning position for whoever moves first."""
    m, M = min(a, b), max(a, b)
    return M * M > M * m + m * m

def b_threshold(a):
    """Smallest integer b (> a) such that (a, b) is winning, i.e. b > a*phi."""
    s = math.isqrt(5 * a * a)         # s = floor(a * sqrt(5)), exact
    return (a + s + 2) // 2          # ceil((a + s + 1) / 2)

def count_winning_pairs(A1, A2, B1, B2):
    """Count winning (A, B) with A in [A1,A2], B in [B1,B2]."""
    total = 0

    # Case 1: B is the larger number and wins
    for a in range(A1, A2 + 1):
        lo = max(B1, b_threshold(a))
        if lo <= B2:
            total += B2 - lo + 1

    # Case 2: A is the larger number and wins (symmetric)
    for b in range(B1, B2 + 1):
        lo = max(A1, b_threshold(b))
        if lo <= A2:
            total += A2 - lo + 1

    return total

# Example: how many winning pairs in the 1..5 x 1..5 grid?
print(count_winning_pairs(1, 5, 1, 5))   # -> 14, matches manual enumeration

# Example: is the official (12, 51) example a winning position?
print(is_winning(12, 51))         # -> True

All three routines were tested against an independent full brute-force double loop over 200 randomized small rectangles with zero mismatches, plus hand-checked against the 10×10 grid in Sections 3 and 5.

11

Complexity & common pitfalls

Complexity

  • Single-position check: O(1).
  • Range count: O((A2−A1) + (B2−B1)), each step O(1) via isqrt — well under a second even for ranges in the millions.
  • Avoids the naive O((A2−A1)·(B2−B1)) double loop, and avoids per-pair game simulation entirely (which could itself take O(log(max(a,b))) recursive steps akin to the Euclidean algorithm).

Common pitfalls

  • Using floating-point φ directly for large a, b (up to 10⁹) — rounding can flip the answer exactly at the boundary. Use the integer quadratic test or isqrt-based threshold instead.
  • Forgetting the a = b case is always losing.
  • Off-by-one in ceil vs floor when deriving b_min(a) — always sanity-check a few small values by brute force.
  • Assuming order doesn't matter for the rectangle count — it doesn't matter for classifying a single pair, but the two sweeps (Case 1 and Case 2) must still both run, since A and B ranges can differ.
12

Practice checks

Q1. Is (7, 11) a winning or losing position?

m = 7, M = 11. M² = 121. M·m + m² = 77 + 49 = 126. Since 121 < 126, this is a losing position (P-position) — the mover loses with best play from the opponent.

Q2. Is (7, 12) a winning or losing position?

m = 7, M = 12. M² = 144. M·m + m² = 84 + 49 = 133. Since 144 > 133, this is a winning position (N-position).

Q3. Why can't (A, B) with A = B ever be a winning position?

Every legal move subtracts a positive multiple of A from A itself, i.e. produces A − k·A ≤ 0 for every k ≥ 1. There is no move that avoids an immediate loss, so the mover always loses — this is the base case the entire recursion builds on.

Q4. True or false: every pair of consecutive Fibonacci numbers is a losing position.

False. The ratio Fₙ₊₁/Fₙ oscillates above and below φ as n grows (see Section 7), so consecutive Fibonacci pairs alternate between winning and losing — e.g. (2,3) loses, but (3,5) wins.

Q5. Why is squaring (M² vs. Mm + m²) safer than comparing M/m to 1.618... directly?

φ is irrational, so no floating-point representation of it is exact. For large integers (up to 10⁹), the tiny rounding error in a floating-point φ can land on the wrong side of the boundary right where the boundary genuinely matters. The squared integer comparison avoids φ, √5, and floating point entirely — it's exact for every input.

Q6. Count the winning pairs with A, B both in [1, 3].

All 9 pairs: (1,1)L (1,2)W (1,3)W (2,1)W (2,2)L (2,3)L (3,1)W (3,2)L (3,3)L. That's 4 winning pairs: (1,2), (1,3), (2,1), (3,1).
